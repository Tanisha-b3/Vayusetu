# Reading data responsibly: observed facts versus possible causes

## Observed versus possible cause
An observed fact is something directly in the readings: a value, a location, a date, a missing cell. A possible cause is a hypothesis. A high reading alone does not prove whether traffic, construction, waste burning, industry or weather caused it. Always label causes as "possible" and say what evidence would confirm them (site inspection, wind direction, time-of-day pattern, complaint logs, nearby sensor comparison).

## Common possible sources (hypotheses only)
Road and construction dust (coarse particles: PM10 high relative to PM2.5). Combustion such as traffic, generators, biomass or waste burning (fine particles: PM2.5 close to PM10). Seasonal factors in north India in late autumn and winter: crop-residue smoke, low wind and temperature inversions that trap pollution near the ground. Local festivals and fireworks. Each is a hypothesis to verify, never a conclusion.

## Using the PM2.5 to PM10 ratio
A high PM2.5/PM10 ratio (for example above about 0.6) is consistent with combustion sources; a low ratio is consistent with dust. This is a hint only. PM2.5 greater than PM10 is physically inconsistent and signals a data error.

## Data reliability rules
Missing values must stay missing. Never estimate or fill a missing reading. Suspicious or invalid readings (negative values, impossible values, PM2.5 above PM10, extreme spikes) must not be used to name hotspots; list them as unreliable. A trend cannot be claimed from a single report; compare with a previous report. One reading per location is a snapshot, not a daily average.

## Hotspot wording
Use "potential hotspot" when readings are at Poor or worse. State the supporting readings. If readings are missing for a location, say hotspot status cannot be assessed rather than assuming it is fine.
